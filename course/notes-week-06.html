    Subsection Chapters 5 and 6

    Note: we are taking a winding path through the textbook because the discussion of energy in chapter 5, though not incorrect, can easily lead to some misconceptions that can come back to bite you when you’re doing the problems. I recommend taking this path, but if you want to read the text straight through, note that there is a big sleight-of-hand happening when you go from section 5.3.2 to section 5.3.3. In particular, the left hand side of (5.15) is not the total work done on the system.
    🔗
    • Sections 5.1 through 5.2 introduce a trick to obtain the solution to the differential equation obtained from Newton’s laws for 1D motion when the force depends only on the position \(x\text{.}\) Section 5.3.2 then formalizes this by defining the concepts of kinetic energy and work done on a particle.
      🔗
      🔗
    • In class, we will talk about how to describe the energy of a two-particle system, and the concept of potential energy will show up. I don’t think the book does a great job of emphasizing that potential energy is a property of a system of interacting objects and doesn’t belong just to one particle. This is likely a new idea for you because in most problems, the source of gravitational potential energy is an interaction with the Earth, and the Earth doesn’t really care about the motion of a small object on its surface. You should wait to read 5.5 and beyond until we have discussed this in class.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Sections 5.6 and 5.7 introduce a graphical method to understand the motion of a particle qualitatively from the just the shape of \(U(x)\text{,}\) no integration required. Energy diagrams are ubiquitous, so look at this section carefully!
      🔗
      🔗
    • Sections 6.1 and 6.3 use energy diagrams to talk about small displacements of an object about an equilibrium point. Small displacements about an equilibrium point are very common, and the fact that we can describe them accurately with the math of simple harmonic motion makes the mass on a spring an incredibly useful model. You’ll see the mass on a spring again in quantum mechanics, solid-state physics, quantum field theory, and beyond.
      🔗
