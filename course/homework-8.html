    2. The Astro-blaster.

    A tennis ball with a small mass m sits on top of a basketball with a large mass M (see figure). The bottom of the basketball is a height \(h\) above the ground, and the tennis ball is standing right on top of it. After the balls are dropped, how high does the tennis ball go? Assume \(M\gg m\) and that the balls are separated by a very small distance, so that they basically collide the moment the basketball reverses its motion. No need to solve the elastic collision problem again from scracth. If you’re up to the challenge, imagine what would happen if you have N balls stacked on top of each other, all satisfying \(m_1\gg m_2 \gg m_3 \gg \cdots \gg m_N\) [this is optional].

    3. The Rocket-Powered Skateboard.

    Consider a rocket-powered skateboard that is about to roll downhill. To escape on the right with the maximum speed, where should it fire a brief impulse (i.e., blast its engines)?
     2 
    This problem is adapted from this paper, and the effect described here (the Oberth effect) is useful for real rockets!
    🔗
    
    A rocket is on level ground ahead of a depression to its right. The depression leads to a hill at higher altitude than the one the rocket starts.
    🔗
    The lowest point at the depression is at heigth \(H\) below the top of the hill, and the rocket starts at height \(h\text{,}\) which is less than \(H\text{,}\) below the hill.
    🔗
    At first glance, it is not obvious why the timing of the impulse would even matter. To have a concrete model for the impulse, let’s say that the rocket, of mass \(M_R\text{,}\) throws a blob of mass \(m\) backwards, which ends up moving with speed \(u\) relative to the rocket.
    🔗
    To avoid any ambiguity: immediately after the blob ejection, someone standing on the skateboard would see the blob traveling away with speed \(u\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Show that the change in the rocket’s velocity immediately after the blast is the same regardless of where you fire the blast.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Show that the total change in kinetic energy (rocket + fuel) also does not depend on where you fire the blast.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Finally, show that the change in kinetic energy of the rocket does depend on where the blast is fired
      🔗
      🔗
    4. To make sure you’re interpreting everything correctly, complete the following sentence: “The total kinetic energy of the system increases by . Mechanistically (or shall we say chemically?), this extra energy comes from , so it makes sense that it’s a fixed amount regardless of when the rocket fires. However, the resulting increase in rocket kinetic energy can be different, because .”
