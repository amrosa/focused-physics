    General Advice and Logistics.

    Logistics: Challenge problems are collaborative. You are required to work in groups. Individual submissions will not be accepted, so please make sure to find a partner ahead of time. Please contact the instructor if you are having trouble finding a partner.
    🔗
    Starting on your own is encouraged, but articulating your arguments, questions, and understanding those of your classmates is one of the key skills this course seeks to develop. If you cannot clearly explain your argument, or are thrown off by questions, your understanding is not solid.
    🔗
    Some tips: The hardest thing to fix is a blank page. Start with drawing a careful picture; writing everything that’s given; everything that’s unknown; the thing you’re looking for; then writing all the things you know are true, labeled by why they are true.
    🔗
    🔗
    Perfectionism reminder: the goal of the challenge problems is to challenge you. This means that they will be hard, and when you turn them in for the first time you may not be sure if your solution is correct. That’s OK: you will get feedback on your solution and you will have a chance to resubmit, taking the feedback into account. Furthermore, we are not looking for perfection (but you should strive for it anyway!). I don’t mean to say you should just throw something at the wall and see if it sticks. Just remember that the challenge is part of the process and it’ll lead to growth.
    🔗
    Sanity checks: Once you have solved the problem, check that your solution makes sense. We will ask you to do this over and over, because it is an important skill to develop. When you submit your design for a bridge, you better be sure it won’t collapse. When you publish a paper with your major theoretical breakthrough or experimental discovery, you want to be sure that you are right. Perhaps a way of thinking about this is as follows: try to find errors in your solution. Are the units right? If I make a particular variable go to zero or infinity, does the answer behave in the expected way? If I plug in reasonable numerical values, do I get a reasonable result?
    🔗
    Academic Integrity: Looking up solutions to this problem to see if it’s already been solved, as well as using AI tools to generate solutions/ideas for the problems, is not allowed. Doing this is not only a serious ethica violation (presenting work that is not your own as if it were), but it short-circuits the valuable learning process of thinking hard about something and refining your thinking until you get it right. If you need help, talk to one of your instructors, or collaborate with another group. You have two chances! Believe in yourself and take it slowly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Challenge Problem Statement.

    A superball is a very “grippy” ball of mass \(M\text{,}\) radius \(R\text{,}\) and moment of inertia \(I_{CM}=\alpha MR^2\text{.}\) When you throw a superball under a table, it will bounce back and return to you. Our goal is to explain this behavior by analyzing the three collisions between the ball and the ground or table. We will assume that the ball does not slip relative to the table in each collision, and we’ll also ignore gravity (throw the ball really hard!).
    🔗
    We can think about each collision as having three parameters: the velocity components \(v_x,v_y\text{,}\) and the angular velocity \(\omega\text{.}\) The collision will then change these to new values. Hint: if the ground and table are infinitely stiff, what should happen to \(v_y\text{?}\) Once you figure this out, then the goal of the problem is really to figure out how the elastic collision with the floor and table change \(v_x,\omega\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Once you solve the collision with the floor, you may then assume that the ball is thrown with no rotation, and then analyze the sequence of three collisions to check that the ball should roughly have reversed its direction of motion after the third impact, once you set \(\alpha=2/5\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Comments for Challenge Problem 5.

    This problem has a lot of algebra. Conservation of energy makes it seem like you have to solve a quadratic equation, but you really don’t have to: there is a trick here, and reducing the collision to two linear equations will make your life easier.
    🔗
    Since the algebra is complicated, do try to find sanity checks for your intermediate steps. Patience is key for solving this problem. If you are 100% certain your equations are perfect, you may use a tool like Mathematica to solve them, but that means you won’t be able to get feedback on what the computer did!
    🔗
    It may seem as though you have to re-do the calculation for the collision of the ball with the table, but there is a trick there involving a transformation of \(\omega\) and thinking about collisions with “back-spin” (the piece of surface about to impact the ground is moving in the opposite direction as the CM) and “top-spin”. Just make sure to be very careful with signs.
