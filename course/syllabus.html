    Course Description.

    Physics 193 is the first part of a two-semester calculus-based introduction to physics, with a co-requisite of Calculus II (Math 132 or its equivalent). The course is an advanced first-year course in classical mechanics, taught at a more sophisticated level than Physics 191. The approach is that of an upper-division physics course, with more emphasis on the underlying formal structure, rather than breadth of topics. The main goal is to provide an in-depth c overage of the physical laws that govern the motion of objects, forces, and forms of energy in mechanical systems, as well as an introduction to special relativity. The course is particularly addressed to students considering a physics or mathematics heavy science/engineering majors. L31 193 students should enroll in a L31 1741 laboratory section. Students may not receive credit for more than one of Physics 117A, 197, 191 and 193.

    Subsection Assessments and Grades

    Subsubsection Grading Philosophy

    Learning requires deliberate practice and engagement in a feedback loop. Deliberate practice means practice with intention, attempting to improve your mastery over specific skills or ideas in a way that is repeatable and meaningful, as opposed to “I’ll read the textbook 10 times and eventually it’ll make sense”.
    🔗
    A feedback loop means trying something, receiving feedback, and using the feedback to practice so you can try again. Long story short, it means working hard and thinking hard. The assignment structure for the course has practice and feedback loops built in so you can try things, struggle productively, and learn from your work. We also firmly believe that you are able to master all the fundamental skills in the class, and so reattempts are built into every course assignment.
    🔗
    The grade mapping for the course will use “checkmarks” or “achievements” instead of traditional points (see grading chart below). This should be easier for you to track, but more importantly, your grade is tied to specific skills, so the answer to the question “how do I improve my grade?” will always be: “practice something until you know how to do it better”.
    🔗
    Since there are no points, all of the grading is binary: “yes” or “no”. That said, we are not expecting perfection. It certainly makes sense to strive to do things perfectly (whatever that means), but we understand that even perfect understanding does not necessarily translate to perfect performance. See the “Expectations on Assignments” document for more details on this.

    Subsubsection Detailed Assignment Descriptions

    • Engagement Credits.
      Taking charge of your own learning and engaging with the content and skills in a college course is a key component of success. This means preparing for class, attending class and participating in in-class activities, doing deliberate practice (homework, office hours, reflection), and more.
      🔗
      Cultivating these habits is an important academic skill, and so activities that require mindful engagement with the course will be awarded engagement credits. Successfully completing these assignments will award you credits that will be tallied on Canvas. These activities are:
      🔗
      • Class preparation: a good-faith effort in completing reading quizzes will award you 2 engagement credits.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Class attendance: coming to class and staying for most of the class session will award you 1 engagement credit.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Student hour attendance: coming to any class help session will award you 1 engagement credit.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Self-reflection: submitting a reflection assignment (open every week on Canvas) will award you 2 engagement credits. Think of these as a make-up for student hours.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • Homework.
      Every week on Monday, a homework assignment will be released covering the content of the week. Problems will have a range of difficulties so you can reflect on your work and the main lessons of each question before moving onto the next one.
      🔗
      The goal of the homework problems is not for us to test your understanding of the material, but for you to test yourself and have a first chance at practicing the week’s material. As such, homework will be graded for completion, but you will still receive detailed feedback on each problem (see the “Expectations on Assignments” document).
      🔗
      Note: if your problem set is significantly incomplete or containing significant physics mistakes, we will ask you to attend office hours/book a meeting with one of the course instructors to discuss the problems before earning credit for that week.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Fundamental Skills.
      Mechanics is a foundational subject in your study of physics. Regardless of where your studies ultimately take you, you will engage with the concepts and techniques in this course repeatedly. This means that there are several skills that need to be mastered before moving forward.
      🔗
      We have called these “fundamental skills”, and you can about them in the “Fundamental Skills” document on Canvas. The 15 fundamental skills will be evaluated in 4 exams. Each skill will be tested in a problem (possibly with a few parts and follow-ups), and each problem will be graded as “Successful” or “Retry”.
      🔗
      There is no partial credit but note the possible grade of “Retry”. If you are not successful initially, you will have time to study and try your hand at the skill again. Since exams include retries, they will be quite long, and we do not expect you to complete an entire exam if you need to retry a few learning targets. Keep this in mind when you decide which problems to do for each exam.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Challenge Problems.
      Physics (every science, in fact) is more than a collection of facts, formulas, and experimental procedures. It is a way of looking at the world and analyzing problems using a conceptual and quantitative framework. Challenge problems are an opportunity for you to think deeply about how the different topics and techniques you will encounter in the class are interconnected.
      🔗
      There will be 5 challenge problems during the semester, which must be completed in pairs. Each challenge problem consists of two steps: a first submission, which will be returned by the grader with feedback, and a revision. Challenge problems are graded as “Successful” or “Needs Revision” (see the expectations page on Canvas for more information).

    Subsubsection Determining Course Grades

    Unlike traditional grading schemes, your grade is determined by your success in the learning targets, challenge problems, and homework completion separately. This means that you cannot use your homework grade to boost your exam grade, for instance. The reason for doing this is clarity: averaging different assignments can make it difficult for you to determine what you are doing well and what needs work.
    🔗
    The learning targets are all listed on Canvas, and you will know after an exam what you did well and what you need to work on. This means that “improving your grade” should always have a clear path: study the topics you had trouble and try again on the next exam. The challenge problems are also evaluated separately from your exams, since they are measuring something different, which is your ability to work in groups and to “stretch” your physics skills.
    🔗
    I know that grades can be a source of stress but do remember that not everything you turn in needs to be perfect. And remember: there are second chances for every assignment! The table below lists the success requirements for each grade level in terms of learning targets, challenge problems, and homework assignments.
    🔗
    Grade modifiers: If you earned over 90 engagement credits and satisfied the requirements for a “base grade”, you will get a + grade. For example: 9 learning targets, 3 challenge problems, and 10 homework assignments, along with 100 engagement credits is a B+. If you have at least one learning target and one challenge problem above the requirement for a particular grade you will earn the next highest minus grade. Example: 10 learning targets, 4 challenge problems, and 10 homework assignments is an A −

    Subsection Additional Recommended Textbooks

    The list below contains additional resources to help you study for this class. There is plenty more to be found online and in the library, but this is a curated list that will help you in this semester and beyond.
    🔗
    • Introduction to Classical Mechanics by D.J Morin. This book is at about the same level as the main text. It has many problems and solutions, and approaches the material creatively.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The Feynman Lectures on Physics by R.P. Feynman, R. Leighton, and M. Sands. This text is now available online for free. This isn’t a textbook, but lots of students appreciate Feynman’s colorful explanation of the material, though some of the language is quite dated.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Physics, 5th Ed by D. Halliday, R. Resnick, and K. Krane. This book is at a lower level than our textbook, but it’s definitely worth having it around if you feel lost at some point and need to take a step back before approaching K&K. Freely available through the library.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Introduction to Special Relativity by R. Resnick. A very clear and methodical approach to special relativity. Worth reading if you find the main text’s tangents too distracting. Freely available through the Internet Archive.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Special Relativity by A.P. French. This book has a good focus on physical intuition. It includes experimental data and has interesting problems. Freely available through the library.

    Subsection General Advice

    This section is also provided as a separate document on Canvas if you wish to have a copy of just this part.
    🔗
    Learning is a messy process. Research on cognitive science tells us that the most effective forms of learning are the ones that require your active engagement and effort, as well as those that confront misconceptions and mistakes head on. As you likely have experienced, struggling through something is not a comfortable process, but it can be very rewarding. I want you to think and work hard on the problems and concepts we will discuss so that you can construct your own understanding of the course topics in your mind, that is there for you when you need it in the future. Here’s some advice from me to help you set yourself up for success.
    🔗
    • Perfectionism: I will often tell you to “aim for flawless solutions”, but please don’t think that everything you submit needs to be perfect. “Aim for flawless” means that once you have solved a problem, you should be able to review your solution and understand its meaning clearly. Every step flows logically from the assumptions you made, there are no guesses in your solution, the mathematics is clear, and your algebraic solution passes basic sanity checks. This is a high bar, and I want you to try and reach it because I believe in you. That said, you are also learning, and it is also my job to give you feedback. I prefer seeing a page full of questions than a blank page, or work that isn’t yours
      🔗
    • Don’t overcommit: I’ve seen first year students compete to see who can take the most credits and sleep the fewest hours. I recommend you use your first semester to adjust to being in college, learn effective study strategies like the ones provided by the Learning Center, and try out new things. This course is 4 credit units, and the university’s general expectation is that one credit unit should take you 2 to 3 hours of work outside of class. I want you to have time to think about physics and to talk to your classmates about physics. Note that I’m not asking you to not be ambitious, just to try and know your limits.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A personal anecdote on overcommitting: I took the equivalent of 25 credits in my second semester of college, and between classes and labs I was in a classroom for about 35h a week. It did not go well for me, but it did get me to focus on physics classes the following term.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Ask for help early and often: If you need help with something, let me know right away. I am here to help you, and I want to help you. If you need an exception with an assignment, or extra help, or something came up, or ( … ), let me know as soon as possible. From a practical perspective, physics builds on itself, so keeping pace with the course is important, and if you wait to tell me that there’s something wrong, it gets harder to help you.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Collaboration: I encourage you to work with other students, form study groups, and collaborate as much as you can. Challenge problems will also require collaboration: individual submissions are not accepted. Collaboration is an important part of how modern science and engineering are done, so it is important to practice those skills from the start. We have also observed that students who work with others have more fun and do better in the course.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Pitfalls of collaboration: It is easy to join a study group and not actively engage in discussing problems, instead just listening to a classmate who is more confident or talkative. You must make sure that everything you turn in represents your understanding and your thoughts. Otherwise, the feedback you will receive from the instructors will not be useful and you might not understand how, for example, your homework assignments translate into exam performance. Start the homework assignment before you meet with other students, so you have your own ideas on how to attempt the problems. When you are working in a group, don’t let one person take over every discussion. Rotate who starts discussion of a problem.
      🔗
      🔗
    • WRITE NEATLY! The graders are instructed not to look for deep meaning in random disorganized scribbles. Clarity of presentation is clarity of thought. You do not need to be verbose; but you do need to be neat and clear.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Late assignments: Challenge problems and reading quizzes have strict deadlines. I will accept late homework if you email me 24h before the homework assignment with a reasonable plan for when the homework will be completed. You don’t have to explain why you’re missing the deadline, but if this is happening often, I reserve the right to ask for a meeting before extending the deadline.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Note-taking: I encourage you to take your notes on paper, rather than on your computer. There is some evidence that this is better for your long-term retention and memory. If you want to have electronic notes, something like a tablet is recommended so you can write instead of type. I will also try to keep the classroom environment as analog as possible since I know screens are distracting and make it harder to focus.
      🔗
      🔗
    • ChatGPT and other LLMs:With all of the above advice in mind, I will recommend that you not use ChatGPT or other LLMs to study for this class. In a sense, LLMs are like the ultimate version of the “very confident classmate”. Whatever input you give to them, they will generate text output that answers the question “What would an answer to this question sound like?”. This is not the same thing as answering the question, and it’s important to know that LLMs make mistakes, and those mistakes can be subtle and ultimately set you back.
      🔗
      More importantly for me, LLMs generate answers quickly, and as such they short-circuit the learning process of thinking hard and constructing understanding in your mind. You read the LLM output, tell yourself “Yeah, that makes sense”, and then you forget it. Evidence on LLM usage in education is still in its early stages, but the picture that seems to be emerging is that reliance on LLMs is detrimental to learning and can lead to dependence on these systems. This goes against the goal of this course of training you to think critically.
      🔗
      All that said, I will not police your LLM usage beyond the basic academic integrity requirements, since I’m not a cop. Copying an assignment on an LLM and submitting the prompt counts as plagiarism. What I actually care about is if you do that, you miss an opportunity to get feedback and grow by reflecting on the feedback. If you do use an LLM, keep this in mind, as well as the effective study strategies mentioned earlier in this document.
      🔗

      To ensure that accommodations may be made for students who miss class, assignments, or exams to observe a religious holiday, you must inform me in writing before the end of the third week of class, or as soon as possible if the holiday occurs during the first three weeks of the semester. For more information, please see the university’s Religious Holiday Class Absence Policy.
