    Objectives

    The purpose of the homework is to:
    🔗
    • Practice applying the concepts and techniques discussed this week
      🔗
      🔗
    • Reveal gaps in your understanding so you know what to study
      🔗
      🔗
    • Enable a productive re-read of the textbook chapter/engagement with other course resources.
      🔗
      🔗
    The homework is both a valuable learning opportunity and a gauge of how well you’re doing. Did you solve all the problems by yourself with no help? Incredible! If that didn’t happen, that’s OK: we’re not expected to get everything right the first time. What you need to do is look carefully at what you needed help with, and then make a study plan, which will likely include solving more problems, to fill in the gaps in your understanding.
    🔗
    🔗
    Instructions: Complete all the exercises below and submit your work on Canvas by the due date.
    🔗

    1. Spacetime Physics 8-3.

    A few notes to help you with each of the parts:
    • Part a) is like a variable mass problem: if you gain momentum \(d\vec{p}\) in time \(dt\text{,}\) the force acting on you is \(\vec{f}=\dfrac{d\vec{p}}{dt}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • As explained in sample problem 8-5, to go from intensity (power per area) to absorbed power, you use the cross section \(\pi r^2\) of the satellite.
      🔗
      🔗
    • You may use, without explanation, that the intensity of solar radiation at distance \(R\) from the sun is \(I(R)=I_* \dfrac{R_E^2}{R^2}\) , where \(I_*\) is the solar constant and \(R_E\) is the Earth-sun distance (150 million km). The quick explanation is that the solar power output is a constant \(P_0\text{,}\) and that power radiates in every direction, so the intensity (power/area) at any distance is \(P_0/4πR^2\text{.}\) You’ll also need the idea from b/c.
      🔗

    3. Impossible Processes.

    Show that the following processes are dynamically impossible. Lots of ways of approaching these: you can try brute-forcing the conservation laws, seeing things in a clever reference-frame, using an elegant 4-momentum argument (or some combination of all three!).
    🔗
    1. A single photon strikes a stationary electron and gives up all its energy to the electron.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A single photon in empty space is transformed into an electron and a positron.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A fast positron and a stationary electron annihilate, producing only one photon.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Uranium Fission.

    A common fission process for uranium is
    \begin{equation*} n+\ce{^{235}_{92}U}→\ce{_{36}^{141}Ba}+\ce{_{56}^{92}Kr}+3n\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The masses of neutron, uranium, barium, and krypton are, respectively, 1.0086u, 234.9934673u, 140.8836846u, and 91.906425u.
    1. How much energy (in MeV) is generated in this process? 1u\(c^2\)=931.5MeV
      🔗
      🔗
    2. As a fraction of the initial mass, how much mass is lost in this process?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. How much energy can be generated in this way from 1kg of Uranium? Compare with the energy generated by burning 1kg of fossil fuels, which is roughly \(50\times 10^6\) J
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Practicing Taylor Expansions.

    You shoot a pion and a muon, both with an energy of 10 GeV, down a 1km tunnel. By how much distance does the muon win? The pion has a mass of 134 MeV, and the muon has a mass of 105 MeV. So note that the gamma factors are quite large (roughly 100 for the muon). Do this in two steps:
    🔗
    1. First, using the binomial expansion \((1+x)^n\simeq 1+nx\text{,}\) valid when \(nx\ll 1\text{,}\) derive an expression for \(\beta\) as a function of \(\gamma\text{,}\) which for you will be valid when \(\gamma \gg 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Now use basic kinematics, as well as any other approximations you see fit, to find out by how much the muon comes out ahead. The lesson here is: series expansions are your friend.
      🔗
      🔗
