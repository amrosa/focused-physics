    The purpose of the homework is to:

      🔗
      🔗
    The homework is both a valuable learning opportunity and a gauge of how well you’re doing. Did you solve all the problems by yourself with no help? Incredible! If that didn’t happen, that’s OK: we’re not expected to get everything right the first time. What you need to do is look carefully at what you needed help with, and then make a study plan, which will likely include solving more problems, to fill in the gaps in your understanding.

    2. Wing-Flapping Frequency of Birds.

    A hovering animal (like a hummingbird) must use its wings to generate a lift force that is equal in magnitude to its weight. It does this by flapping its wings and pushing air downwards. This process can be complicated, but our goal is to obtain a simple estimate of how often a bird must flap its wings: this will be a frequency f (beats per second)
     1 
    This problem is adapted from this paper, which has some data. You should check it out!
    🔗

    (a)

    Find an approximate formula for what this lift force should depend on. Clearly define any parameters you introduce, such as the length \(L\) of the wings. At this point, its fine to keep your lift force in terms of a speed instead of trying to introduce the frequency.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now figure out how to relate, approximately, the speed you used in the previous part to the wing beating frequency \(f\) (hint: frequency has units of 1/time)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Let us now make the gross estimate that all animals have roughly the same density \(\rho\text{.}\) Using your assumption, find how the frequency f scales with animal size: that is, you should find an expression of the type \(~{f=CL^n}\text{.}\) The constant \(C\) isn’t particularly important (it can depend on lots of things that we aren’t taking into account here!), but the power n can be compared to experiment. This is an example of an allometric scaling relation
